Overview

Here I briefly update on my progress on finalizing my multi-variate meta-analytical Distributed Lag Non-linear Model (DLNM), adapted from Gasparrini and colleagues paper (1) . First, I will outline my sensitivity analysis and rational in selecting certain parameters for modelling (e.g. seasonality, autocorrelation, cross basis degrees of freedom and model family). Then I report preliminary outputs for total patient outcomes only, since this model needs more input before proceeding to other outcomes. Finally, I give my suggestions in moving forward and outstanding questions to the team.

As a reminder, my research questions for this analysis are as follows:

  • Lag-response: How is the effect of a single exposure at time \(t\) distributed across future time points (lags)? E.g. If it’s hot today, does the health impact occur immediately, or does it show up in 2–3 days?

  • Exposure-response: How do different levels of the exposure variables affect the outcome? E.g. How does the risk of hospital admissions change when temperature increases from 20°C to 30°C?

  • Do the exposure–lag effects vary across vulnerable groups? E.g. Are children under 5 or the elderly more sensitive to specific temperatures or lag duration compared to the general population?

  • Do the exposure–lag effects vary across location? E.g. Are some locations more at risk due to their unique geographies (i.e. elevation) or population profiles (i.e. population density)?

Please note that my initial results focus on the first 2 because I am still optimizing parameters.

Sensitivity Analysis

Controlling for long-term trends and seasonality

Previous studies show that annual hospital admission patterns vary irregularly across locations, making explicit time functions (e.g., month- or year-specific indicators) unsuitable for adjusting long-term trends and seasonality, as they assume constant or repeating temporal patterns. We therefore used a flexible smoothing function of time to account for unmeasured time-varying confounders. In the sensitivity analysis, I varied the df of time splines from 1 to 12 df per year and evaluated model fit via AIC scores, as illustrated below. Here, we can see the model fit gradually decreases until the df reaches 10 per year, and then remains stable.

Controlling for autocorrelations related to disease transmission

Hospital admissions encompass many health outcomes, including infectious and non-infectious diseases. Unlike non-infectious diseases, the transmission of infectious diseases may cause strong residual auto correlations, even when seasonal and long-term trends are controlled for. Autocorrelation plots indicated that there was substantial autocorrelation at one and 2 weeks, so we tested lags from 1-14. When adding an autoregressive term with a lag of 1, autocorrelation disappeared and no further tests were required. This is further reflected in the pooled AIC plots below.

Choices of the flexibility of the lag of the exposure-response relationships

Identifying the appropriate range of exposure-response lags is key to obtaining accurate estimations. Since hospital admissions address a multitude of infectious and non-infectious diseases – each with different long- and short-term transmission routes that are variably influenced by meteorological factors – it will not be possible for us to specifically control for all possible lag periods. Considering this, we chose to examine a set period between 0 to 14 days, because we are primarily interested in short-term, acute weather-induced health effects. This was fit as an unconstrained distribute lag model with a df of 4, which is widely applied across DLNM studies.

We found that higher lag df’s were preferred across most exposures. However, in the interest of less complex models and consistently, I chose to use a lag of df=6 for all exposures since these had sufficiently low AIC values across variables.

Choices of the flexibility of the shape of the exposure-response relationships

With the lag structure identified, we then assessed the shape of each exposure-response relationship and appropriate flexibility. Prior research thus suggests that the relationships between my weather phenomena and hospital admissions are non-linear. That said, with the exception of temperature, most studies of meteorological variables opt for a simple form of representation and assume a linear relationship with few lags. Therefore, we tested linear and non-linear relationship for all predictors. Non-linear models were described by natural cubic splines with df’s varied from 3 to 8. the figure below summarises AIC results across weather variables.

We found that the model preferred linear representations of almost all variables. However, based on previous literature, we know this is not accurate and the second lowest AIC values were ns | df=3. Therefore we opted to use natural splines with df=3 for all known non-linear exposures (i.e. temperature, rainfall and relative humidity) and only PM2.5 was represented linearly.

Choices of the statistical models

Up until this point, all tests have been conducted with quasi-Poisson models for the sake of parsimony. However, both the quasi-Poisson and negative binomial models are commonly used in healthcare modelling to address overdispersion issues. However, each model assumes a different relationship of variance to the mean. While both account for additional noise in the data, the quasi-Poisson assumes a constant multiplier, whereas the negative binomial allows the multiplier to vary across groups (Ver Hoef JM, Boveng PL 2007).

We assessed how each model effected the shape of the temperature and total hospital admission relationship (see below). The differences were negligible, so we opted for the quasi-Poisson model for our final model to minimize complexity.

Preliminary Results

This section is summarized as follows:

  • The analysis includes data for 8 hospitals across Himachal Pradesh between 2015 and 2020.

  • Overall, it seems that higher temperatures decrease total hospital admissions and dry conditions (low humidity) increase admissions. No other effects are observed at this level.

  • Regarding lags, cold weather (10% and 25%) increase admissions around lag 10, whereas hot conditions (75% and 90%) decrease admissions on the day of occurrence (lag 0). Dry weather (10% and 25%) increase admissions the day of occurrence (lag 0), whereas humid conditions (75% and 90%) decrease admissions on the day of occurrence (lag 0). No other effects are observed at this level.

  • This is similarly reflected in exposure levels. For instance, we observe a ~4% increase in hospital admissions risk at low temperatures and 10-days after occurrence. Increase air pollution may also increase hospital admission risk, but the effect sizes are negligible.

  • Rainfall, which we would expect to have more impact given converation with partners, showed no effects what so ever.

Notes on interactions

All interaction tests I have run suggest multiple interactions between almost all combinations of our weather variables. I do not think it is feasible of appropriate to bring these into the main models of this analysis, but we will need to address this somehow before proceeding to the TSGC analysis. If we focus on just two variables (e.g. temperature and rainfall), we can maybe assess changes in estimates across deciles of the relevant interacting variables for that exposure, identified a-priori from literature. For instance:

  • Effects of cold events compounded by high humidity

  • Effects of Heat events compounded by high air pollution and humidity

  • Effects of Precipitation events compounded by high winds

Of course air pollution effects on health are further compounded by humidity, winds and rainfall, but I think this project is already very complicated and air pollution should not be a primary exposure of interest.

Thoughts

Personally, I think the model is currently too complicated and needs to be simplified. We are interested in how extreme weather impacts health care demand, and I think the number of exposures needs to be downsized. Additionally, we are not currently accounting for cumulative weather effects which may be obscuring current estimates, particularly for rainfall. Therefore my thoughts and questions are as follows:

  • We should focus on temperature and rainfall as primary non-linear exposures. The other variables we have identified (i.e. humidity, wind and air pollution) can remain in the main model but only linear controls for now.

    • Question: Humidity should be treated as non-linear, but does that make sense if it is just a control? I am not sure and would appreciate another opinion.

  • I am worried that cumulative and repeat exposures are not being adequately captured and potentially skewing the model. To address this, I would like to add a 7-day moving average/sum to weather variables.

    • Question: Do I need to worry about the implications of using a moving average when interpreting the model lags? I don’t think it will be an issue but wanted a second opinion.

  • From the model outputs, I will be able to identify binary thresholds of weather events and pinpoint appropriate lag points for the TSGC model. However, I could take this further and create an index for different weather events, namely cold spells, heat waves and heavy precipitation. This will be based on the exposure-response risk levels identified by the model, but could also incorporate interacting weather exposures. However, this would require the DLNM analysis to be extended to include weather-specific interaction models, i.e. one for each weather events and known interactions identified a-priori.

    • Question: Is this too much for a single analysis? Probably, and I can side step it but think there may be value in investigating these interactions.
