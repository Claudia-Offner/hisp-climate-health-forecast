Overview

Here I briefly update on my progress on finalizing my multi-variate meta-analytical Distributed Lag Non-linear Model (DLNM). First, I will outline my exposure selection and sensitivity analysis, providing an explanation for variable inclusion and model parameters (e.g. seasonality, autocorrelation, cross basis degrees of freedom and model family). Then I report preliminary outputs for overall and city specific results, followed by seasonal and population group models Finally, I give my suggestions in moving forward and outstanding questions to the team.

As a reminder, my research questions for this analysis are as follows:

  • Lag-response: How is the effect of a single exposure at time \(t\) distributed across future time points (lags)? E.g. If it’s hot today, does the health impact occur immediately, or does it show up in 2–3 days?

  • Exposure-response: How do different levels of the exposure variables affect the outcome? E.g. How does the risk of hospital admissions change when temperature increases from 20°C to 30°C?

  • Do the exposure–lag effects vary across vulnerable groups? E.g. Are children under 5 or the elderly more sensitive to specific temperatures or lag duration compared to the general population?

  • Do the exposure–lag effects vary across location? E.g. Are some locations more at risk due to their unique geographies (i.e. elevation) or population profiles (i.e. population density)?

Non-parameter correlation test

Spearman correlation tests indicate that the link between total patients and various atmospheric exposures is small in magnitude (Figure 2). Temperature–particularly mean, max and wet-bulb temperature–have the highest correlation with hospital admissions (0.21), followed by wind speed (-0.19). Links to rainfall, diurnal range, air pollution and relative humidity are tenuous. However, it is possible that associations are obscured by spatial-temporal trends, so I further assessed associations via a XGBoost feature importance model, controlling for such factors.

XGBoost feature importance model

Across all locations, the XGBoost model indicated that spatial-temporal factors like autocorrelation, weekday, time, population density and elevation outweighed the importance of weather exposures (Figure 3). However, among the weather exposures, temperature–particularly maximum and wet-bulb temperature– ranked most important, followed by wind speed, air pollution, relative humidity and rainfall. These weather effects were very small relative to other model factors and slightly differ from the correlation tests.

It is possible that feature importance differed across locations. To asses this, we ran the XG boost algorithm for each site subset with hyper parameters optimized for each location. We found that feature importance varied considerably across sites (Figure 4), with patterns in feature importance difficult to identify. To delve deeper, we counted the frequency at which each feature appeared in the top and bottom 5 of the feature gain list (Table 1). From these tests, it is clear that temperature and wind speed are important factors, but it is difficult to draw more decisive conclusions on other weather exposures. Based on literature and partner input,we know rainfall is one of the most challenging weather extremes in the area and that temperature induced health outcomes are exacerbated by air pollution and humidity. So for now, these factors will remain in my model.

  • 7-day cumulative precipitation (mm)

Sensitivity Analysis

The following sensitivity analysis is conducted to select DLNM parameters suitable for pooled effects from 8 cities. Decisions are guided by theory and pooled AIC model criteria and pooled ACF plots, where appropriated. A one-way analysis strategy was applied, evaluating one decision at a time while keeping others fixed. Model choices were assessed sequentially, following their relative importance and the logical order of the time-series modelling process, with each step informed by the previous one. Lag and shape parameter tests specifically assessed the impacts of temperature, rainfall and wind speed as predictors of hospital admissions.

Note: This section is still in progress because I am finding it difficult to select parameters objectively.

Controlling for long-term trends and seasonality

Previous studies show that annual hospital admission patterns vary irregularly across locations, making explicit time functions (e.g., month- or year-specific indicators) unsuitable for adjusting long-term trends and seasonality, as they assume constant or repeating temporal patterns. We therefore used a flexible smoothing function of time to account for unmeasured time-varying confounders. In the sensitivity analysis, I varied the df of time splines from 1 to 12 df per year and evaluated model fit via AIC scores, as illustrated below. Here, we can see the model fit gradually decreases until the df reaches 10 per year, and then remains stable. However, the difference between df’s 8-10 is negligible, so we will opt for a simpler model fit at df=8.

Controlling for autocorrelations related to disease transmission

Hospital admissions encompass many health outcomes, including infectious and non-infectious diseases. Unlike non-infectious diseases, the transmission of infectious diseases may cause strong residual auto correlations, even when seasonal and long-term trends are controlled for. Autocorrelation plots indicated that there was substantial autocorrelation at one and 2 weeks, so we tested lags from 1-14. When adding an autoregressive lag of 1, autocorrelation improved but did not entirely disappear. Furthers test were conducted to assess the combination of AR1 and lags 2:14 to see if correlation could be improved. It was found that a model with lags 1 and 13 had the best AIC criteria, but this was comparable to models with lags1 and 5 or 6. ACF plots indicated that lags 1 and 6 reduced autocorrelation the most so this model was adopted.

Note: There is still a lot of autocorrelation in the data. Maybe differencing techniques would help, but that significantly changes the model setup and interpretation.

Choices of the flexibility of the lag of the exposure-response relationships

Identifying the appropriate range of exposure-response lags is key to obtaining accurate estimations. Since hospital admissions address a multitude of infectious and non-infectious diseases – each with different long- and short-term transmission routes that are variably influenced by meteorological factors – it will not be possible for us to specifically control for all possible lag periods. Considering this, we chose to examine a set period between 0 to 14 days, because we are primarily interested in short-term, acute weather-induced health effects. This was fit as an unconstrained distribute lag model with a df of 4, which is widely applied across DLNM studies.

We found that low df’s of 3 were preferred across most exposures, except relative humidity. However, in the interest of less complex models and consistency, I chose to use a lag of df=3 for all exposures since these had sufficiently low AIC values across variables.

Choices of the flexibility of the shape of the exposure-response relationships

With the lag structure identified, we then assessed the shape of each exposure-response relationship and appropriate flexibility. Prior research thus suggests that the relationships between my weather phenomena and hospital admissions are non-linear. That said, with the exception of temperature and relative humidity, most studies of meteorological variables opt for a simple form of representation and assume a linear relationship with few lags. Therefore, we tested linear and non-linear relationship for all predictors. Non-linear models were described by natural cubic splines with df’s varied from 3 to 8. the figure below summarizes AIC results across weather variables.

We found that the model preferred linear representations of almost all variables. However, based on previous literature, we know this is not accurate so we further compared the shape of known non-linear variables (i.e. temperature and relative humidity) for each df. These plots show that the wiggling of the shape of temperature- and humidity-admissions relationship increased with increased df of natural cubic splines. However, the relationship generally remained stable when df exceeded 4, which is in agreement with previous studies. However, this is not consistent with out AIC comparison criteria. Therefore, to find a middle ground, I opt to use a natural splines with df=4 for all known non-linear exposures (i.e. temperature and relative humidity) and all other variables (i.e. rainfall, PM2.5 and wind speed) were represented linearly.

Choices of the statistical models

Up until this point, all tests have been conducted with quasi-Poisson models for the sake of parsimony. However, both the quasi-Poisson and negative binomial models are commonly used in healthcare modelling to address over dispersion issues. However, each model assumes a different relationship of variance to the mean. While both account for additional noise in the data, the quasi-Poisson assumes a constant multiplier, whereas the negative binomial allows the multiplier to vary across groups (Ver Hoef JM, Boveng PL 2007). We assessed how each model effected the shape of the temperature and total hospital admission relationship (see below). The differences were negligible, so we chose the quasi-Poisson model for our final model to minimize complexity.

Preliminary Results

Analysis 1: Overall

This analysis includes data for 8 hospitals across Himachal Pradesh between 2015 and 2020. Please note that rainfall is log transformed for modelling, which is why the city specific estimates look pushed to oneside in plots. All exposures were modeled on a relative scale which greatly improved model interpretability. Location specific percentile look ups can be found with the supplementary tables attached with this report. These results are summarized as follows:

  • Looking at cumulative effects across lags, the only weather exposure to have a significant effect on admissions is precipitation. However this effect is of very small magnitude and may be considered negligible. Please note that air pollution and wind speed were excluded from subsequent plots since they were consistently insignificant.

  • The lag-response plots suggest that cold temperatures elicit an significant increase in relative risk of admission at lag 14. Warm temperatures seem to decrease admissions at the same lag, but increase risk of admission at lag 5, but only at the 90th percentile. Humid conditions initially decrease admission the first 3 lags, but suggest a very slight increase after a week. Dry conditions have no effect. Precipitation does vary over time, but the plot axes need to be fixed so this is more visible.

  • These relationships are similarly reflected in the exposure-response plots. For instance, we observe a ~4% increase in relative risk of hospital admissions 14-days after the occurrence of cold temperatures dipping below the 20th percentile. Conversely, we see a ~2% increase in hospital admissions risk 5-days after the occurrence of high temperatures exceeding the 90th percentile. On the day of occurrence, humidity exceeding the 80th percentile decreases admissions by ~4%. However, 10 days after exposure, a 1% increase is observed at the same percentile. Precipitation only has a significant effect between lags 5 and 10, but the magnitude is effectively zero.

City-specific differences

Using the same model fit as above, we can examine the city specific differences, plotted against the pooled results. The plots below provide the cumulative exposure-response effects over time for each weather exposure. Here we find:

  • Wet-bulb temperature: Una seems to be particularly susceptible to cold temperatures below the 20% percentile, with a notable ~50% increase in relative risk of hospital admissions, with wide but significant confidence intervals. The only other city with a significant cumulative effect is Kullu, which demonstrates a notable ~30% decrease in relative risk of admissions when the 80th percentile is exceeded.

  • Precipitation: Only Solan and Una show a marginal increase in relative risk of hospital admissions due to precipitation. There are no other observable effects for other locations.

  • Relative humidity: There are no observable effects for relative humidity.

Case study: Una & Solan

Considering the findings above, it is worth examining a specific city to understand how these cumulative exposures vary across lags. Here we use Una and Solan as case studies to understand how their respective exposure-response relationships vary across lags.

  • Una: Cold weather increases the relative risk of hospital admissions by ~6% on the day of occurrence. No other effects were observed at other lags or for other weather variables.

  • Solan: Cold weather also increase the RR of admissions for Solan, but at lag 14 and by ~12%. Warm weather increased admissions by 4% at lag 5 and humid conditions decreased admissions the day of occurrence. No other effects were observed at other lags or for other weather variables.

Analysis 2: Seasonal differences

After conducting the initial primary analysis provided above, it was noted that these relationships likely differ by season. To assess this, I ran two additional models, which were separated into winter/pre-monsoon months (January-May) and Monsoon/Post-monsoon months (June-December). Model parameters remained the same as above. Our results are as follows:

  • Winter: As with previous results, cold temperature had the greatest effect at lag 14, increasing the RR of admission by almost 5%. Precipitation also increased the RR at lags 5 and 10, but the magnitude of effects are negligible. Unlike the above results, dry conditions appear to increase the RR of admission by 3% at lag 10 when relative humidity is below 20%.

  • Summer: Dry conditions increased the RR of admission by 5% on the day of occurrence when relative humidity was below 20%. Similarity to winter, precipitation also increased the RR at lags 5 and 10 with negligible negligible. No other effects were observed at other lags or for other weather variables.

Analysis 3: Population differences

Finally, we ran population specific models to understand gender and age differences. Here I present results for specific outcomes based on significant lags identified in the overall model. Model parameters remained the same as previous analyses. Our results are as follows:

  • Temperature (Lag=14): Women were the population group most notably affected by cold weather and experienced a 4% increase in RR of admissions when temperatures were below the 20th percentile.

  • Precipitation (Lag=5): No significant effects were observed.

  • Relative humidity (Lag=10): Humid conditions had no effect on children aged under 5 and aged 5-14. All other population groups experienced and increase in RR of admission to to humid conditions at fairly similar levels.

Discussion

Overall, the greatest increased in hospital admission risk occurs when temperature recedes below the 20th percentile. While there are observable effects for precipitation and relative humidity, the magnitude of effects are incredibly small and therefore negligible. There were no effects observed for air pollution and wind speed. Exposure-lag-response distributions vary widely across cities and exposures, which may explain why the pooled effects are so small. Interestingly, seasonal model’s indicate that people are more susceptible to hot, cold and precipitation extremes in the winter, but not the summer. Women are more susceptible to cold weather, but no other population effects were observed across exposures.

Some, but not all, of these effects may be explained by existing literature and discussions with partners:

  • Exisiting literature has established links between health outcomes and cold, hot, humid and dry weather conditions, as well as interacting effects with air pollution, which can explain these observed effects. However, based on conversations with partners, we know that heavy rainfall and consequent landslides are the greatest weather extreme of concern for Himachal Pradesh. Furthermore, heat and air pollution extremes are not frequently observed. Considering this, our finding are not at odds with what partners have flagged as relevant weather extremes, but the magnitudes of the effects do not reflect ground truth.

  • When separated by season, exposure distributions change but not in a way that one would expect. For instance, we would expect hot extremes and heavy rainfall to effect hospital admissions more in the monsoon months but we see the opposite. When heat and precipitation exceed the 80th percentile in the winter month, that is when we see an increase in RR of admissions. This could make sense when we consider the effects of unseasonable weather, which would naturally be more disruptive in seasons where they are not anticipated by the population.

  • It is also well established in literature that women are most susceptible to weather extremes, confirming these observed effects. However, we would also expect to see more of an effect on children and adults over 65 which is not the case.

Ultimately, I would like to use these plots to establish percentile based classifications of weather extremes for each city, which can then be used for interaction tests and forecasting. However, there are a number of methodological considerations that still require exploration and input form the supervisory team. Here are some of my current thoughts:

  • This model is still very complicated and I am not entirely sure if the parameters fit are the most appropriate. Interpreting the results based on literature won’t be an issue but knowing how reliable the results are and whether to trust them is where I keep getting stuck.

  • There is still substantial autocorrelation remains in the model. Differencing approaches may minimize these but it would change the model type and interpretation. Additional support on how to address this would be appreciated.

  • It is normal for city-specific estimates to differ but, considering our small sample size, it may not be beneficial to pool the effects all together since they seem to cancel each other out. Perhaps pooling by elevation group (e.g. above vs below 1000m) or climate group may minimize the considerable divergences. However, this will not change the fact that we have a sample size of ~10 hospitals, which is not a lot to pool and begs the question of whether this makes sense.

  • Other DLNM papers have applied moving exponential averages at certain lags to all weather exposures. I can see the benefit of this approach in making cumulative weather effects more prominent but like to know if this is sound reasoning prior to applying.
